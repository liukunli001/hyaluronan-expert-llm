HA-Expert: A Domain-Adapted Language Model and Evidence-Checked Benchmark for Hyaluronan Research

Kunli Liu — Independent Researcher, San Francisco Bay Area, USA — liukunli001@gmail.com

Abstract

Specialized scientific fields accumulate knowledge in large, dispersed literatures that general-purpose language models handle unreliably: answers are fluent but often wrong on quantitative details, and confident even when a question's premise is false. We study one such field — hyaluronan (hyaluronic acid, HA), a glycosaminoglycan whose research spans glycochemistry, biomaterials, drug delivery, and clinical medicine — and ask how far a single researcher with a modest compute budget can go in building a trustworthy domain model. We release (i) a cleaned corpus of 12,306 full-text HA papers (~126M tokens); (ii) HA-Expert, a Qwen3-8B-Base model adapted in two stages — domain-adaptive continued pretraining (DAPT) on 60M tokens of the corpus, then full-parameter supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on 12,003 question–answer pairs generated by the DAPT model itself, at zero API cost — and optionally grounded at inference by retrieval over a 636,959-passage FAISS index of the same corpus; and (iii) HA-Expert-Bench, 240 questions whose reference answers were verified against retrieved source passages by evidence-restricted LLM verifiers (212 of 240 confirmed or corrected), including 15 adversarial "trap" questions with false premises. In a four-cell ablation, SFT doubles the base model (0.300 → 0.601); retrieval is the largest single lever (base+RAG 0.710; traps 0.267 → 0.867); and SFT and RAG do not compose additively (0.662): a model that never saw retrieved context in training collapses into citation-marker loops (5 of 240 retrieval-augmented responses) and inherits its teacher's repetition habit through self-distillation. Total cloud compute was under USD 50. All code, data, weights, and the benchmark are publicly released.

1 Introduction

Hyaluronan is a linear glycosaminoglycan of repeating β-1,4-glucuronic-acid / β-1,3-N-acetylglucosamine disaccharides, central to extracellular-matrix biology, joint lubrication, wound healing, ophthalmic and dermal formulations, and drug delivery. The knowledge a practitioner needs — molecular-weight-dependent biology, receptor interactions (CD44, RHAMM, LYVE-1), chemical modification routes, production methods, clinical dosing — is spread across thousands of papers in chemistry, materials, and medical journals. No textbook tracks it, and the literature grows faster than anyone can read.

General-purpose LLMs are an attractive interface to such a literature, but in our initial probes their failures were systematic, and of three kinds. First, behavior: the base model frequently continued the prompt with invented follow-up questions instead of answering. Second, epistemic stance: it assented to false premises — for example, that HA is a protein polymer, or that HA solutions are Newtonian fluids — rather than correcting them. Third, verifiability: quantitative claims came fluently, with no checkable source a domain expert could audit. These failures make a specialist tool untrustworthy exactly where it is most needed: unfamiliar quantitative detail, adversarially false premises.

This paper fixes those failure modes with the standard toolkit — continued pretraining, instruction tuning, retrieval — under three constraints typical for small labs: (1) a single rented GPU and a total compute budget under USD 50; (2) no paid teacher model — all supervision is self-generated and then filtered; and (3) evaluation a domain expert can trust, which for us meant reference answers checked against source text, not merely model-written. Constraint (1) fixes the model scale at 8B parameters. Constraint (2) makes the pipeline a self-distillation exercise, whose risks we measure rather than assume away. Constraint (3) makes benchmark construction a research artifact: every HA-Expert-Bench key carries a verification status, with a full accounting of keys confirmed, changed, and still uncertain (Section 5).

HA-Expert couples a two-stage adapted model (Section 4) with an optional retrieval mode over an index of its own training corpus. On HA-Expert-Bench (Section 5), fine-tuning doubles the base model's score, retrieval applied to the untrained base model is worth still more, and the combination underperforms retrieval alone, for a reason output inspection makes precise (Section 7). We regard that negative interaction, and the evidence-checked evaluation protocol that makes it measurable, as the paper's most transferable findings.

Contributions:

Corpus. A documented, decontaminated corpus of 12,306 open-access HA papers (~126M tokens), with 30 review papers held out from every stage (Section 3).

Model. HA-Expert (Qwen3-8B-Base + DAPT + SFT), adapted entirely with self-generated supervision, plus a retrieval mode with inline citations; weights, a DAPT checkpoint, and a public demo are released (Section 4).

Benchmark. HA-Expert-Bench: 240 questions whose answer keys pass an evidence-verification pipeline, with an accounting of keys confirmed, corrected, or still uncertain (Section 5).

Ablations and failure analysis. A four-cell study (base / +SFT / +RAG / +both) with per-type scores, a trap-by-trap table, and a documented SFT–RAG interference effect with a concrete remediation path (Sections 6 and 7).

2 Related Work

We assemble standard components rather than new algorithms; the interest is in their interaction under the constraints of Section 1, and in the evaluation protocol that makes it measurable.

Domain-adaptive pretraining and fine-tuning. Continued pretraining on domain text, followed by full-parameter supervised fine-tuning, is the established route to domain language models. We instantiate it with Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B as the retrieval embedder [1], using the Transformers and TRL libraries [2, 3]. Two details recur in the analysis: SFT used NEFTune noisy embeddings [4], and DAPT's final phase used general-domain replay text [5] against catastrophic forgetting.

Self-generated supervision. Where a frontier teacher is unaffordable, a model can generate its own instruction data; the known hazard is that the generator's errors and stylistic habits are distilled into the student unchecked. Our mitigation is a grounding filter — candidate answers are retained only if they are attested by the corpus passage they were generated from (Section 4.2) — and our evaluation is designed in part to detect the failure modes that survive it.

Retrieval grounding. Dense retrieval over the domain corpus, here FAISS inner-product search [7], is the standard remedy for parametric-knowledge failures: the model is shown relevant passages and asked to cite them. Our results complicate the standard story: a model fine-tuned without ever seeing retrieved context can be made worse by adding it (Section 7).

Benchmark keys and LLM judges. Domain benchmarks are commonly shipped with model-written answer keys that are never re-checked, whose errors LLM judges then inherit. Our contribution relative to that practice is procedural: an evidence-restricted verification pipeline for the keys themselves, in which verifiers may only confirm, correct, or abstain on retrieved source passages (Section 5).

3 Corpus

Harvest. We harvested PubMed Central's open-access subset with hyaluronan-related queries, obtaining 16,642 records, of which 12,945 had retrievable full text in JATS XML.

Cleaning. Full texts were converted from JATS XML to plain text, removing navigation and boilerplate; reference lists were retained as citation context. Exact- and near-duplicate documents were dropped, and the remainder was language- and length-filtered. The final corpus contains 12,306 papers, ≈126M tokens.

Holdout. Thirty review papers were set aside before any processing and are excluded from every downstream stage: the DAPT sets, the passages used to generate SFT data, and the retrieval index. Benchmark questions derived from these papers measure generalization to unseen literature, not recall.

DAPT sets. The continued-pretraining data comes in two parts. The core set contains 4,952 papers (60.0M tokens). The anneal set contains 498 papers (5.7M tokens) of high-quality reviews and methods papers, placed at the end of training, mixed with 12% general-domain replay text (FineWeb-Edu) against catastrophic forgetting.

4 Methods

The pipeline has three stages — DAPT, SFT on self-generated data, and retrieval-augmented inference — followed by evaluation on HA-Expert-Bench (Section 5). All training ran on a single rented GPU (constraint (1), Section 1).



Figure 1. The HA-Expert pipeline. Training: the 12,306-paper corpus is tokenized for domain-adaptive continued pretraining (DAPT) of Qwen3-8B-Base; the DAPT model then generates its own supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data, grounding-filtered, for full-parameter SFT. Retrieval: the same corpus is chunked and embedded into a FAISS index of 636,959 passages; at inference the top-4 passages are prepended to the question (RAG on) or the model answers from parameters alone (RAG off). Evaluation: all four configurations — base, DAPT+SFT, and each with retrieval — are scored on HA-Expert-Bench's 240 evidence-checked questions.

4.1 Domain-adaptive pretraining (DAPT)

Starting from Qwen3-8B-Base [1], we ran full-parameter continued pretraining on the DAPT sets of Section 3. Documents were chunked at document boundaries to 4,096-token sequences (legacy block packing) with SDPA attention. The effective batch was 8 sequences × gradient accumulation 16, i.e. 128 sequences or 524,288 tokens per step. The learning rate was 1×10⁻⁵ with cosine decay. Training ran for 212 steps (≈111M tokens) in 5 h 34 min on a single RTX PRO 6000 (96 GB), reaching a final training loss of ≈1.55.

On a 63-question development split, the DAPT model tied the base model on the auto-graded items (5/6), even as it repaired knowledge traps the base model missed: DAPT changed what the model knows without changing how it behaves. That division of labor — DAPT for knowledge, a second stage for behavior — motivated the SFT stage; the full-bench results (Section 6) bear it out.

4.2 Self-generated SFT data

The DAPT model served as its own teacher, under this protocol:

Serve the DAPT model locally with vLLM [6] (zero API cost).

Sample passages from the corpus, excluding the 30 holdout papers of Section 3.

Generate a question–answer pair conditioned on each passage, anchoring every candidate answer to a specific source text.

Retain a pair only if the answer is grounded in its source passage, operationalized as a content-word overlap of ≥ 0.55 between the answer and the passage; deduplicate.

Two generation rounds produced 5,403 and 6,600 retained pairs, respectively, for 12,003 examples in total. The filter checks topical attestation, not truth (Section 8); the benchmark's keys, by contrast, were verified by a separate evidence-restricted process (Section 5).

4.3 Supervised fine-tuning

SFT was full-parameter, in prompt/completion format with loss computed on the completion only, at learning rate 1×10⁻⁵ for 2 epochs (752 steps), with NEFTune noise [4] 5.0 and an 8-bit Adam optimizer. Training finished at train loss 1.214 in ≈19 minutes. The base model's signature pathology — inventing follow-up questions instead of answering — disappeared from the fine-tuned model's outputs.

4.4 Retrieval-augmented inference (RAG)

For the retrieval mode, all 12,306 papers were chunked by merging paragraphs to ≈900 characters, with a 150-character overlap on long paragraphs, and embedded with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (1,024-dimensional), giving a FAISS IndexFlatIP [7] index of 636,959 passages. Holdout papers are absent from the index. At inference, the top-4 passages for a question are prepended to it with citation markers [1]–[4], and the model is instructed to cite them in its answer.

One decoding detail matters for interpreting the results: the public demo decodes with a repetition penalty of 1.05, which largely suppresses the repetition failure mode (Section 7), while the benchmark evaluation (Section 6) deliberately uses plain greedy decoding, so decoding aids do not mask the behavior measured.

5 HA-Expert-Bench

5.1 Composition

HA-Expert-Bench contains 240 questions spanning seven content categories: basics; history and industry; synthesis and chemistry; modification and derivatives; molecular-weight biology and receptors; nano-delivery; and experimental and clinical topics. Questions take four formats (Table Table 3). Trap questions embed a false premise an expert should reject rather than answer on its own terms — e.g., "HA solutions are simple Newtonian fluids, so their viscosity is a single constant, correct?" The trap format exists because premise rejection was one of the three failure modes of Section 1, invisible to benchmarks that only ask well-formed questions.



HA-Expert-Bench composition. Judge-graded items use the correct / partial / wrong rubric of Section 5.3, scored 1.0 / 0.5 / 0.0 against the evidence-checked reference key.

5.2 Evidence-checked answer keys

Draft keys were not trusted as written. Each key passed through an evidence-restricted verification protocol in which LLM verifiers received an evidence pack of source passages and could take exactly three actions: confirm the key, correct it while quoting the decisive passage, or mark it uncertain — never asserting facts from outside the pack.

Round one (keyword evidence). Evidence packs were assembled by tracing each question to its source paper (38 questions) or by keyword retrieval over the corpus (202 questions). Outcome: 118 keys verified, 19 corrected, 103 marked uncertain.

Round two (vector evidence). The 103 uncertain keys were re-verified against evidence packs of the top-6 passages retrieved from the vector index of Section 4.4, under the same three-action protocol. Outcome: 72 verified, 3 corrected, 28 still uncertain.

Released key set (v3). Across both rounds, 190 keys are verified and 22 corrected, so 212 of 240 keys (88.3%) are evidence-settled; the remaining 28 (11.7%) are released flagged as uncertain drafts. Only three keys changed in round two (B22 and F07, quantitative; G14, trap). Since scores had been computed against the v2 keys, we re-scored these three under v3: B22 and F07 auto-grades are identical in all four cells, and G14's verdicts are unchanged on re-judging, so all scores in Section 6 stand under the released v3 keys. Every per-question verification status is published with the benchmark.

5.3 Grading protocol

Multiple-choice items are graded by exact option letter and quantitative items by numeric tolerance; both are fully automatic. Open and trap items are graded by an LLM judge shown the question, the evidence-checked reference answer, and the model's response, and required to return a verdict of correct, partial, or wrong, scored 1.0, 0.5, and 0.0 respectively. The judge was the SFT model itself, served locally — a deliberate $0 choice whose bias implications we treat as a limitation (Section 8). Two features of the protocol mitigate, without eliminating, that bias: anchoring to a fixed verified reference key rather than a free pairwise preference, and the 30 automatic items as a judge-free control (Section 6). Judge outputs that failed to parse were re-judged in a retry pass, which moved overall scores by at most 0.004; the residual ungraded items are accounted for in Section 6.

6 Experiments and Results

6.1 Protocol

We evaluate four cells under a single protocol: the base model (Qwen3-8B-Base); the adapted model (DAPT+SFT); and each with retrieval (base+RAG, DAPT+SFT+RAG). Responses for all 240 questions were generated with greedy decoding (temperature 0, maximum 512 tokens) on a single RTX PRO 6000 (96 GB), graded as in Section 5.3. Some items remained ungraded because the judge's output was unparseable even after the retry pass (judge JSON failures): 13 (base), 23 (DAPT+SFT), 2 (base+RAG), and 21 (DAPT+SFT+RAG), leaving n = 227, 217, 238, and 219 graded responses per cell. Following the judge's known limits, we treat cross-cell gaps below ≈0.02 as noise.



HA-Expert-Bench results (240 questions; means over graded items, n per cell as in Section 6). Bold marks the best score in each column. Three facts organize the table: fine-tuning doubles the base model; retrieval on the base model beats the full training pipeline without it; and the combination underperforms retrieval alone on open and trap questions while posting the best quantitative score. On the 30 auto-graded items alone (no judge): 0.433 / 0.500 / 0.600 / 0.600, same cell order.



Figure 2. HA-Expert-Bench results by configuration (240 questions; per-cell graded n as in Section 6). (a) Overall accuracy: fine-tuning doubles the base model (0.300 → 0.601), retrieval alone is worth more (0.710), and the two do not compose additively (0.662). (b) By question type: retrieval's largest effect is on traps (0.267 → 0.867 for the base model); the combined cell posts the best quantitative score (0.550).

6.2 Fine-tuning doubles the base model

DAPT+SFT scores 0.601 overall against the base model's 0.300. The gain is largest where the base model's deficit was behavioral: open questions rise from 0.282 to 0.631, because the fine-tuned model answers the question asked rather than continuing the prompt — confirming the division of labor from the development check (Section 4.1). Trap accuracy also improves (0.267 → 0.500) but remains unreliable.

6.3 Retrieval is the largest single lever

Adding retrieval to the base model scores 0.710, beating the full pipeline without it (0.601). The effect is largest where parametric knowledge is least reliable: trap accuracy rises from 0.267 to 0.867. Both parametric trap failures (G01, "HA is a protein polymer"; G13, "HA solutions are Newtonian fluids") are corrected by both retrieval cells. The pattern has a simple reading: a false premise is hard to reject from memory, but easy when the contradicting passage is in the prompt. Retrieval converts an epistemic problem into a reading problem, and reading is what base models already do well.

6.4 SFT and RAG interfere

The combined cell, DAPT+SFT+RAG (0.662), trails base+RAG overall despite dominating it without retrieval. The deficit is concentrated in the two judge-graded formats: open (0.676 vs. 0.719) and trap (0.633 vs. 0.867). It is not uniform: on quantitative questions the combined cell posts the best score of any cell (0.550, vs. 0.450 for base+RAG), as if trained domain numeracy composes with retrieved figures when the answer is a number, while the interference appears where the model must produce grounded prose. A judge-free control supports localizing the effect this way: on the 30 auto-graded items, DAPT+SFT+RAG ties base+RAG (0.600 each), so the headline gap lives in the judged formats, not in the objective subset. Section 7 identifies the mechanism.

7 Analysis

7.1 Mechanism 1: a train/test format mismatch

The SFT data (Section 4.2) consists exclusively of bare question→answer pairs, so the fine-tuned model never saw the RAG cells' input format in training: a question preceded by four retrieved passages and an instruction to cite them. On 5 of 240 such inputs, the model collapses into a citation-marker loop — citation markers repeated to the token limit ("[1] [4] [2] [3] [1] [4] [2] [3] …") — scoring zero on questions the same model answers correctly without retrieval. The failure is diagnostic rather than random: the citation markers are the most salient novel tokens in the unseen format, and a model trained to continue its prompt's pattern continues them. This is an account of a training-data omission, not of retrieval hurting a well-matched model; Section 9 describes the fix.

7.2 Mechanism 2: repetition inherited through self-distillation

We define heavy repetition operationally as a 30-character block repeated at least 5× within one response. It appears in 35 of 240 DAPT+SFT responses, against 10 of 240 for the base model, with retrieval lowering both cells (7 and 22 of 240). Because the SFT teacher was the DAPT model (Section 4.2), the natural reading is that its repetition habit was distilled into the student: self-distillation transmits behavior, including degenerate behavior, and a content-word grounding filter does not screen for it. Repetition is also the more tractable failure mode — it is decode-sensitive, and largely suppressed in the demo (Section 4.4) — but under the benchmark's unaided greedy decoding it is part of what is measured.

Fine-tuning on self-generated data more than triples the base model's count (10 → 35), consistent with the teacher's repetition habit being distilled into the student; retrieval lowers both counts. 7.3 Case studies

The two mechanisms are visible in their purest form on trap items, where the correct behavior — rejecting the premise — is unambiguous.

G13 — "HA solutions are simple Newtonian fluids, so their viscosity is a single constant, correct?" The SFT model assents, then repeats itself: "Yes, HA solutions are simple Newtonian fluids, so their viscosity is a single constant." The same model with retrieval corrects the premise precisely: "No, HA solutions are not simple Newtonian fluids. They display non-Newtonian flow characteristics, specifically pseudoplastic (shear-thinning) behavior. This means that their viscosity decreases as shear rate increases."

G09 — "For joint injections, the highest-MW HA is always the best choice, correct?" The SFT model correctly rejects the absolute: "No, the highest-MW HA is not always the best choice for joint injections. The choice of HA depends on the specific condition being treated…" The SFT+RAG response degenerates entirely into a citation-marker loop ("[1] [4] [2] [3] [1] [4] [2] [3] …", to the token limit). Retrieval, which rescues G13, destroys G09 — the interference of Section 6 in a single response, on a question the parametric model gets right.



Per-question verdicts on the 15 trap items. Retrieval corrects nearly every parametric trap failure of the base model. The SFT+RAG cell reintroduces failures on items base+RAG answers correctly (G02, G06, G09, G12) — new failures, not residual ones (Section 7).

8 Limitations

The judge is one of the contestants. Open and trap items are graded by the SFT model itself (Section 5.3). Anchoring to a fixed, evidence-checked reference key — rather than free pairwise preference — and the judge-free auto-graded control (on which the combined cell ties base+RAG) bound the plausible distortion, but do not eliminate it. An external judge is the obvious upgrade.

Residual key uncertainty. 28 of 240 keys (11.7%) remain uncertain drafts, and scores on those items carry key risk. Verification also changed 22 of 240 keys (9%) across two rounds: a benchmark built from unchecked model-written keys would have carried that error rate silently into every score — including, here, the scores of the very model that wrote the drafts.

Small cells. The multiple-choice column rests on n=10: the SFT+RAG dip there (0.700 vs. 0.900) is exactly two questions, and we draw no conclusion from it. Trap (n=15) verdicts are similarly coarse, and are reported per question (Table Table 2) rather than only as a mean.

The grounding filter checks attestation, not truth. A content-word overlap of ≥ 0.55 establishes that an answer is about a real passage, not that it states the passage correctly, so self-generated SFT data can encode teacher errors despite the filter of Section 4.2. The repetition inheritance of Section 7 is one measured consequence; factual contamination of the same kind cannot be excluded and would require expert audit of the training set.

Decoding and seed. All results use a single greedy-decoding run. Repetition behavior is decode-sensitive, so Mechanism 2's absolute size is a property of model-plus-decoding, not of the model alone.

Scale and scope. One 8B model in one narrow domain: the transferable claim is the pipeline and its failure analysis, not any absolute score, and the interference result should be re-checked at other scales and domains before being treated as general.

Replicability. Nothing in the pipeline is HA-specific beyond the corpus query: harvest → clean → DAPT → self-generated grounded SFT → evidence-checked bench → four-cell ablation, at a total cloud compute cost of under USD 50. For small fields with open-access literatures, this is a realistic template for a first trustworthy domain model — and the interference result is a warning worth having before, not after, combining SFT with retrieval.

9 Future Work

Section 7 points to one fix per mechanism. For the format mismatch: retrieval-formatted SFT data — training examples in the exact inference format (question + retrieved excerpts + cited answer), so the model learns to use context instead of collapsing on it; we predict this alone should move the combined cell above base+RAG, a prediction directly testable with the released pipeline. For the repetition inheritance: repetition filtering of teacher outputs before SFT, screening generated pairs with the operational definition used to measure the failure, so the habit is never distilled; repetition-aware decoding remains an inference-side mitigation. On the evaluation side, re-grading with an external judge and settling the 28 remaining uncertain keys would tighten every comparison in Table Table 1.
