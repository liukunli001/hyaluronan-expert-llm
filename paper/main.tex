\begin{abstract}
General-purpose large language models answer questions about
specialized scientific fields fluently but unreliably: they err
on quantitative details and remain confident even when a question rests
on a false premise. We ask what a single researcher, without
datacenter resources, can do about one such field ---
hyaluronan (hyaluronic acid, HA). From a corpus of 12,306 HA papers
($\approx$126M tokens), we build HA-Expert by domain-adaptive
continued pretraining of Qwen3-8B-Base (DAPT; 60M core + 5.7M anneal tokens) and
full-parameter supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on 12,003
question--answer pairs that the DAPT model itself generated, retained
by a grounding filter; at inference, retrieval over a 636,959-passage FAISS
index can ground its answers. To measure trustworthiness rather than
fluency, we construct HA-Expert-Bench: 240 questions whose keyed
answers were confirmed or corrected by LLM verifiers shown only
retrieved evidence (212 of 240 settled), including 15 adversarial
trap questions built on false premises. In a four-cell ablation,
fine-tuning doubles the base score (0.300 $\rightarrow$ 0.601) and
retrieval is the largest single lever (base+RAG 0.710; traps 0.267
$\rightarrow$ 0.867), yet the two do \textbf{not} compose additively
(0.662). The shortfall traces to two mechanisms: a train/test format
mismatch that drives a model never trained on retrieved context
into citation-marker loops (5 of 240 responses), and repetition
inherited through self-distillation. The pipeline is domain-agnostic
apart from the corpus query, so the recipe transfers to other fields
defined by a literature (heparin and other glycosaminoglycans;
bioconjugation chemistry). The released model already turns novel
research ideas into structured plans; adding corpus-scale novelty
checking and evidence-checked evaluation would extend the same
approach to ideation support and first-pass reviewing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Hyaluronan is a linear glycosaminoglycan built from repeating
disaccharide units of $\beta$-1,4-linked glucuronic acid and
$\beta$-1,3-linked $N$-acetylglucosamine. Working knowledge of the
field --- molecular-weight-dependent biology, receptor interactions,
chemical modification, production, and clinical dosing --- is dispersed
across thousands of papers in chemistry, materials science, and
medicine.

General-purpose LLMs promise a natural interface to such a literature,
but our probing uncovered three systematic failure modes:
\emph{behavior} --- the base model continues prompts with invented
follow-up questions instead of answering; \emph{epistemic stance} ---
it assents to false premises, such as that HA is a protein polymer or
that HA solutions are Newtonian fluids; and \emph{verifiability} --- it
states quantitative claims with no checkable source. Two of the three
appear in a single response: asked whether HA solutions are Newtonian,
the base model first invents follow-up questions (``Also, how do I
calculate the viscosity of a 10\% w/w solution of HA in water at
25$^\circ$C\ldots?'') and then assents --- ``Yes, HA solutions are
simple Newtonian fluids, so their viscosity is a single constant.''

We address these failures with the standard toolkit --- continued
pretraining, instruction tuning, and retrieval --- under three
constraints that define the small-lab setting: (1) a single rented GPU;
(2) no paid teacher model, so supervision is self-generated and then
filtered; and (3) evaluation that can be trusted: reference answers
\emph{checked against source text} rather than merely model-written.
Constraint (2) makes the training a self-distillation exercise; we
therefore measure its risks rather than assume them away.

Our contributions are the corpus, the model
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), and the benchmark --- and the headline
result is negative. In the four-cell ablation, fine-tuning doubles the base
model's score and retrieval on the untrained base is worth still more,
yet their combination underperforms retrieval alone
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We assemble standard components rather than new algorithms; the
interest lies in their interaction. Continued pretraining followed by
full-parameter SFT is the established route to domain language models,
and domain-specific systems can outperform larger general
or biomedical models on specialized tasks: Tariq et
al.~\cite{tariq2024}, for example, trained a 124M-parameter
prostate-cancer model with a domain-specific tokenizer and UMLS-guided
masked-term prediction, and it surpassed the three-times-larger BioGPT
on clinical information prediction. Here we instantiate that route
with Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B~\cite{qwen3}, using the
Transformers and TRL libraries~\cite{transformers,trl}, NEFTune
noise~\cite{neftune}, and general-domain replay~\cite{fineweb}.
Self-generated instruction data risks distilling the generator's errors
and habits unchecked; we guard against this with a grounding filter
(Section~\ref{sec:sft-data}) and an evaluation designed to expose
whatever survives it. Dense retrieval (FAISS~\cite{faiss}) is the
standard remedy for the resulting knowledge gaps. Finally, domain
benchmarks commonly ship model-written answer keys that no one
re-checks, so any key error silently propagates into every judged
score; our keys, by contrast, are evidence-verified
(Section~\ref{sec:bench}).

\section{Corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

\textbf{Harvest and cleaning.} We harvested PubMed Central's
open-access subset, obtaining 16,642 records, of which 12,945 came
with retrievable JATS XML full text. Plain-text conversion, exact and near
deduplication, and language and length filtering left \textbf{12,306
papers ($\approx$126M tokens)} (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}).

\textbf{Holdout.} Thirty review papers were set aside \emph{before}
any processing and excluded from every downstream stage (DAPT, SFT
data generation, and the retrieval index), so that questions derived
from them measure generalization rather than recall.

\textbf{DAPT sets.} From the corpus we drew two DAPT sets: a
\emph{core set} of 4,952 papers (60.0M tokens) and an \emph{anneal set}
of 498 papers (5.7M tokens) consisting of reviews and methods papers,
presented last during training, alongside 12\% FineWeb-Edu replay.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The system has three stages --- DAPT, SFT on self-generated data, and
retrieval-augmented inference --- followed by evaluation on
HA-Expert-Bench (Section~\ref{sec:bench}; Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline});
everything runs on a single rented GPU.

\subsection{Domain-adaptive pretraining (DAPT)}
\label{sec:dapt}

We performed full-parameter continued pretraining of
Qwen3-8B-Base~\cite{qwen3} on the DAPT sets of
Section~\ref{sec:corpus}. Documents were chunked into 4,096-token
sequences (legacy block packing) and trained with SDPA attention, an
effective batch of 128 sequences (8 $\times$ gradient accumulation 16;
524,288 tokens per step), and a learning rate of $1\times10^{-5}$ with
cosine decay, for 212 steps ($\approx$111M tokens) in 5\,h 34\,min on a
single RTX PRO 6000 (96\,GB), reaching a final loss of $\approx$1.55.
On a 63-question development split, DAPT tied the base model on
auto-graded items (5/6) yet repaired trap questions the base model
had failed --- evidence that it changed what the model \emph{knows} rather
than how it \emph{behaves}, and the motivation for the SFT stage that
follows.

\subsection{Self-generated SFT data}
\label{sec:sft-data}

The DAPT model served as its own teacher, in four steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The DAPT model was served locally with vLLM~\cite{vllm}.
\item Passages were sampled from the corpus, excluding the 30 holdout
papers (Section~\ref{sec:corpus}).
\item For each passage, the model generated one question--answer pair
anchored to that passage.
\item Only pairs whose answer was \emph{grounded} in the passage
(content-word overlap $\geq 0.55$) were retained; the set was then
deduplicated.
\end{enumerate}
Two generation rounds yielded 5,403 and 6,600 retained pairs ---
\textbf{12,003 examples} in total --- filtered for topical
attestation, not truth (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Supervised fine-tuning}
\label{sec:sft-train}

We fine-tuned the full model in prompt/completion format, with loss on
the completion only: learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, two epochs (752
steps), NEFTune noise~\cite{neftune} of 5.0, and an 8-bit Adam
optimizer, reaching a final training loss of 1.214 in $\approx$19
minutes. After SFT, the base model's signature pathology ---
inventing follow-up questions --- disappeared.

\subsection{Retrieval-augmented inference (RAG)}
\label{sec:rag}

All 12,306 papers were chunked by merging paragraphs into
$\approx$900-character windows (150-character overlap for long
paragraphs) and embedded with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (1,024-dimensional)
into a FAISS IndexFlatIP~\cite{faiss} index of \textbf{636,959
passages}; the holdout papers are absent from the index. At inference,
the top-4 passages are prepended to the question, marked [1]--[4],
which the model is instructed to cite. Decoding differs by setting:
the demo applies a repetition penalty of 1.05, which largely
suppresses the repetition failure mode (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}),
whereas the benchmark uses plain greedy decoding, so that decoding
aids cannot mask the behavior being measured.

\section{HA-Expert-Bench}
\label{sec:bench}

\subsection{Composition}

HA-Expert-Bench contains 240 questions spanning seven content
categories (basics; history/industry; synthesis/chemistry;
modification/derivatives; molecular-weight biology/receptors;
nano-delivery; experimental/clinical topics) and four formats
(Table~\ref{tab:benchcomp}). \emph{Trap} questions embed a false
premise that an expert should reject, testing the premise-rejection
failure of Section~\ref{sec:intro}.

\subsection{Evidence-checked answer keys}
\label{sec:keys}

Each draft key passed an evidence-restricted verification protocol:
verifiers were shown only an evidence pack and could \emph{confirm}
the key, \emph{correct} it (quoting the decisive passage), or mark it
\emph{uncertain}. In round one --- packs traced to source papers for
38 questions and keyword-retrieved for 202 --- 118 keys were verified,
19 corrected, and 103 left uncertain. Round two re-examined the 103
against top-6 vector-retrieved passages (Section~\ref{sec:rag}): 72
verified, 3 corrected, 28 still uncertain. The released v3 set thus
contains 190 verified and 22 corrected keys --- \textbf{212 of 240
(88.3\%) evidence-settled} --- with 28 (11.7\%) flagged uncertain. Only
three keys changed in round two (B22, F07, G14), and re-scoring those
three under v3 changed no auto-grade and no verdict.

\subsection{Grading protocol}
\label{sec:grading}

Multiple-choice items are graded by exact option letter and
quantitative items by numeric tolerance. Open and trap items are
graded by an LLM judge, which is shown the question, the
evidence-checked reference answer, and the response, and returns
correct / partial / wrong (1.0 / 0.5 / 0.0). The judge was the SFT
model itself, served locally at no cost (for the resulting bias, see
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Two safeguards limit the risk in this design:
grading is anchored to the fixed, verified keys, and the 30
automatically graded items serve as a judge-free control
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). Outputs that could not be parsed
were re-judged in a retry pass, which moved scores by at most 0.004.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}

We evaluate four cells: the base model (Qwen3-8B-Base) and the adapted
model (DAPT+SFT), each with and without retrieval. All responses were
generated with greedy decoding (temperature 0, at most 512 tokens) on
a single RTX PRO 6000 (96\,GB). Items that remained unparseable after
the retry pass stayed ungraded --- 13 (base), 23 (DAPT+SFT), 2
(base+RAG), and 21 (DAPT+SFT+RAG) --- leaving graded samples of $n =
227$, $217$, $238$, and $219$, respectively. Differences below
$\approx$0.02 should be read as noise.

\subsection{Fine-tuning doubles the base model}

DAPT+SFT scores 0.601 against the base model's 0.300
(Table~\ref{tab:main}, Figure~\ref{fig:results}). The gain is largest
where the base model's deficit was behavioral: on open questions,
accuracy rises from 0.282 to 0.631, as the model now answers the
question asked instead of continuing the prompt (cf.\
Section~\ref{sec:dapt}). Trap accuracy also improves (0.267
$\rightarrow$ 0.500) but remains unreliable.

\subsection{Retrieval is the largest single lever}

Base+RAG scores 0.710, above the full pipeline without retrieval
(0.601). The effect is largest precisely where parametric knowledge is
least reliable: trap accuracy rises from 0.267 to 0.867, and four of
the base model's eight parametric trap failures (G01, G11, G13, G14)
are corrected by both retrieval cells. A false premise is hard to
reject from memory, but easy to reject with the contradicting passage
in the prompt.

\subsection{SFT and RAG interfere}

DAPT+SFT+RAG (0.662) trails base+RAG, despite dominating it without
retrieval. The deficit concentrates in the judged formats --- open
(0.676 vs.\ 0.719) and trap (0.633 vs.\ 0.867) --- but is not uniform:
the combined cell is the best on quantitative questions (0.550 vs.\
0.450). On the 30 auto-graded items, the two cells \emph{tie} (0.600
each), which places the headline gap in the judged formats, not the
objective subset.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Mechanism 1: a train/test format mismatch}

The SFT data (Section~\ref{sec:sft-data}) contain only bare
question$\rightarrow$answer pairs, so the fine-tuned model never
encountered the RAG input format --- a question preceded by four
retrieved passages and a citation instruction. On 5 of 240 such inputs
it collapses into a \emph{citation-marker loop} (``[1] [4] [2] [3] [1]
[4] [2] [3] $\ldots$''), scoring zero on questions it answers correctly
without retrieval. The failure is diagnostic. The citation markers are
the most salient novel tokens in an unseen format, and a model trained
to continue the pattern of its prompt continues them. This is a
\emph{training-data omission}, not evidence that retrieval harms a
well-matched model; Section~\ref{sec:future} describes the fix.

\subsection{Mechanism 2: repetition inherited through self-distillation}

\emph{Heavy repetition} (defined in Figure~\ref{fig:failures})
appears in 35 of 240 DAPT+SFT responses, against 10 of 240 for the
base model; retrieval lowers both counts (7 and 22 of 240,
respectively). Because the teacher \emph{was} the DAPT model itself
(Section~\ref{sec:sft-data}), its repetition habit was distilled into
the student: self-distillation transmits even degenerate behavior, and
a content-word grounding filter does not screen for it. Repetition is
decode-sensitive --- it is largely suppressed in the demo
(Section~\ref{sec:rag}) --- but the benchmark's unaided greedy
decoding measures the model as trained.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{The judge is one of the contestants.} Open and trap items are
graded by the SFT model itself (Section~\ref{sec:grading}). Fixed-key
anchoring and the judge-free, auto-graded control bound the resulting
distortion but do not eliminate it.
\textbf{Residual key uncertainty.} Twenty-eight of 240 keys (11.7\%)
remain uncertain drafts. Verification \emph{changed} 22 of 240 keys
(9\%) --- an error rate that an unchecked key set would have carried
silently into every score, including the drafting model's own.
\textbf{Small cells.} Multiple choice rests on $n=10$: the SFT+RAG dip
(0.700 vs.\ 0.900) is two questions, and we draw no conclusion from it.
Trap verdicts ($n=15$) are reported in Table~\ref{tab:traps}.
\textbf{Attestation, not truth.} The grounding filter's overlap
threshold ($\geq 0.55$) shows that an answer is anchored to a real
passage, not that it renders the passage correctly, so the SFT data
can encode teacher errors (Section~\ref{sec:sft-data}); factual
contamination --- the counterpart of the repetition inheritance of
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} --- cannot be ruled out without an expert
audit.
\textbf{Decoding and scale.} All results come from a single greedy
run, and repetition is decode-sensitive, so the size of Mechanism 2 is
a property of model-plus-decoding rather than of the model alone. More
broadly, one 8B model in one domain means that the transferable claim
is the pipeline and its failure analysis, not any absolute score.
\textbf{Replicability.} Only the corpus query is HA-specific; the
remaining pipeline (harvest $\rightarrow$ clean $\rightarrow$ DAPT
$\rightarrow$ self-SFT $\rightarrow$ bench $\rightarrow$ ablation) is a
template for small fields with open-access literatures --- and its
warning is worth heeding \emph{before} combining SFT with retrieval.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Each mechanism suggests a direct remedy. For the format mismatch, we
will train on \emph{retrieval-formatted SFT data} --- examples
presented in the exact inference format (question, retrieved excerpts,
cited answer) --- which we predict will move the combined cell above
base+RAG. For the repetition inheritance, we will apply
\emph{repetition filtering} to teacher outputs, screening candidate
pairs by the failure's operational definition before training. On the
evaluation side, we will re-grade the benchmark with an external judge
and settle the 28 uncertain keys.

Beyond hyaluronan, we see three directions. First, \emph{other
domains}: the pipeline is domain-agnostic apart from the corpus query
(Section~\ref{sec:corpus}), so the same recipe applies to other
fields defined by a literature, such as heparin and other
glycosaminoglycans or bioconjugation chemistry. Second,
\emph{ideation}: the released model already proposes novel research
ideas and expands them into structured plans with hypotheses and
experimental methods; adding corpus-scale novelty checking would
turn this capability into a practical research tool. Third,
\emph{review}: a model with a verified command of a field's
literature could serve as a first-pass reviewer of new submissions,
checking their claims against that literature --- a use that, like
ideation, requires evidence-checked evaluation before it can be
trusted.
